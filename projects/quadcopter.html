01 — Overview

Why a search-and-rescue drone

It began with a simple, personal motivation: my home country, Turkey, sits across several major fault lines. Post-disaster search-and-rescue teams need small, resilient remotely piloted aircraft that can get into collapsed structures where people can't. I set out to design a quadcopter frame around that mission — one that was light enough to fly efficiently, tough enough to survive rough landings, and simple enough to build with accessible manufacturing methods.

The full reasoning and requirements are laid out in the original design proposal, written before any CAD work started.

Read the Design Proposal
02

Design Process — Generative Design & Key Features

The body was built around Fusion 360's generative design tools: instead of sketching a frame and thinning it out by hand, I defined the load cases, keep-out zones, and mounting points, then let the solver propose structures that minimized mass while meeting the load requirements. That process is what drove the frame's organic, lattice-like appearance — every strand of material is there because it's carrying load, not because it looked right on paper.

That approach removed 370 g from the frame compared to a conventional solid design, while keeping enough structure to hit a 4:1 thrust-to-weight ratio once the motors and propellers were sized against it.

  • FEATURE

    Generative-type mesh

    Enhances structural stability while limiting total weight and staying aesthetically clean — the mesh pattern follows the frame's real load paths rather than being decorative.

  • FEATURE

    Component tray

    A dedicated tray to place and stabilize the electrical components, with wire-routing openings and realistic component models built into the CAD so fit was checked before anything was printed.

  • FEATURE

    Landing gear

    Protects the motors and propellers on touchdown and is shaped to dissipate landing impact rather than transmitting it straight into the frame.

  • FEATURE

    Camera mounting points

    Built using surface design tools, with a motion study run to confirm the camera's tilt-and-pan motion wasn't obstructed by the surrounding structure.

02.1 — Verifying Motion

Camera mount, checked in motion

The camera mounting points were built with Fusion 360's surface design tools rather than solid extrudes, which gave more control over the mount's exact profile around the gimbal hardware. Before finalizing the geometry, I ran a motion study to sweep the camera through its full tilt-and-pan range and confirm nothing in the surrounding frame clipped or restricted its travel.

03

Static Stress Analysis & Design Optimization

Once the body design was complete, I ran a static stress analysis to validate it under the drone's operating and landing loads, then used the results to optimize the frame — thickening regions that showed elevated stress concentrations and continuing to remove material where the model showed margin to spare.

The Von Mises stress plot below shows the frame under load: stress stays low and well-distributed across the arms, with the expected concentrations at the motor mounts and central body where loads transfer into the structure.

04

Manufacturing

The frame was printed in nylon 6 — a material that holds up to repeated impact far better than the more common PLA or PETG, which matters directly for a drone whose job is to survive hard landings. The generative geometry was compatible with FDM printing without needing significant support material in the load-bearing arms.

To get more experience designing for other manufacturing processes, I later adapted the same concept into a CNC-machined aluminum version, cut on a waterjet. That build is documented as its own project, since the design constraints changed substantially between the two methods.

05

Engineering Decisions

A few of the decisions that shaped the final design:

  • MASS

    Generative design over manual topology reduction

    Rather than sketching a frame and manually thinning it, I let Fusion 360's solver generate structure directly from the load cases and keep-out zones — a faster way to find an efficient load path than iterating by hand, and the reason the final frame removed 370 g versus a conventional layout.

  • STRUCTURE

    Stress-driven material distribution

    The static stress analysis wasn't just a final check — its results fed back into a second pass of the geometry, adding material at concentration points and removing it where the model showed margin, rather than treating FEA as a pass/fail gate at the end.

  • MATERIAL

    Nylon 6 for impact tolerance

    For the 3D-printed build, nylon 6 was chosen specifically for its impact and fatigue resistance compared to more common FDM materials — directly relevant for a frame that's expected to absorb hard landings in the field.

  • MANUFACTURING

    Building the same concept two ways

    Producing both a 3D-printed and a CNC-machined version of the same frame concept was a deliberate choice — it's a direct comparison of how one design intent has to change shape when the manufacturing process changes underneath it.

Final Result

The completed frame, printed in nylon 6 with all four motors, propellers, and the electronics bay installed. The generative lattice is visible throughout the central body, where it carries load between the arms while keeping the printed part light.
