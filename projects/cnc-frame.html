Sole Designer

01 — Overview

Same mission, different manufacturing method

After finishing the 3D printed quadcopter, I wanted more direct experience designing for a completely different manufacturing process. Generative, lattice-heavy geometry that prints beautifully in nylon doesn't translate to a flat sheet-cutting process — so this project meant re-thinking the frame from the ground up around what a waterjet can actually produce: 2D profiles cut from flat stock, without the freeform 3D lattice of the printed version.

The goal wasn't to out-perform the printed frame — it was to build real fluency in designing for subtractive, sheet-based manufacturing, and to understand how the same design intent has to change shape when the process underneath it changes.

02

Toolpath Generation & Simulation

For this build I selected an OMAX waterjet cutter with a 1.5 mm waterjet tool. Because a waterjet removes material along a 2D profile rather than a freeform volume, the frame's arms, motor mounts, and cable-routing slots all had to be laid out as clean, cuttable outlines — no overhangs or internal lattices to worry about, but every profile and lead-in had to be planned so the toolpath would cut cleanly in one pass.

03

Design-for-Manufacture Considerations

  • GEOMETRY

    Flattening a 3D lattice into a 2D profile

    The printed frame's strength came from a freeform, generatively-optimized lattice. A waterjet can only cut through-profiles in flat stock, so the arm geometry, mounting bosses, and cable slots had to be re-derived as a single flat outline that still routed load sensibly between the motor mounts and the center body.

  • TOOLING

    Sizing features to the 1.5 mm jet

    Every internal corner, slot, and small feature had to stay compatible with what a 1.5 mm waterjet stream can actually cut — internal corners come out with a fillet governed by the jet diameter, not a sharp corner, so those tolerances were built into the profile from the start rather than fixed after the fact.

  • PROCESS

    Single-pass toolpath planning

    Because waterjet cutting is comparatively slow per linear inch, the cut order and lead-in points were planned to minimize repositioning and keep the part accurately located in the stock through the full cut.
